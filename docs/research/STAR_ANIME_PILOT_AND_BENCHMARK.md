# Original pilot and production comparison

Everything in this section is a development proposal. The characters, working title, script, shots and cue windows are original planning material, not approved casting or finished media. The accompanying JSON explicitly contains no selected takes or measured benchmark results.

## 1. The first scene: Afterlight Couriers

Working title: **Afterlight Couriers**. Pilot scene: **Tomorrow's Return Address**. Target: 90 seconds, exact 24 fps, 2,160 timeline frames, two visible adult characters, one off-screen dispatcher and one rooftop environment with two ledges.

Premise: a night courier service delivers messages sent from possible futures. Every delivery can change the future that sent it. The long-term emotional problem is not simply stopping a disaster: it is deciding what to tell people when knowing their future might change them.

**Miko, 20:** resourceful, quick to commit and reluctant to reveal fear. A compact forward silhouette, short dark hair, rust-red cropped courier jacket, reinforced gloves, a small right-ear receiver and a battered satchel with a folded cargo strap. Her competence is real; her habit of withholding difficult information hurts trust. Her humor comes from dodging the emotional part of a question.

**Ren, 22:** careful, observant and unexpectedly brave when another person needs help. A taller, more vertical silhouette, indigo jacket with pale sleeves and practical shoes. He names a risk plainly, then does the useful thing. His rhythm should leave room for a dry response without making him a perpetual joke machine.

**Omi, adult dispatcher:** off-screen for this test. Calm, concise radio delivery; the world feels larger because another competent person is working nearby. No visual design is required for the first scene.

Visual proposal: crisp, variable line weight; controlled shadow shapes; blue-violet evening with a small area of warm shelter light; painted atmosphere over a stable layout. Use a distinct silhouette and expression vocabulary for each character. Spend the main animation effort on the rescue and the quiet reaction to the message.

### Geography and prop rules

Establish Miko on the left roof and Ren on the right roof before the jump. The destination stairwell is behind Ren. Preserve left-to-right travel until both reach safety. The satchel strap is detached, thrown across and checked as a safety tether before Miko jumps. The parcel remains one object and its label never changes. Ren's anchor hand and the tether's attachment point must remain consistent.

For the layout test, use a 2.2-metre gap and a 5-metre unfolded cargo strap. Its loop is on Miko's left wrist; her right arm holds the parcel. Ren clips the far end to a fixed anchor ring on his roof, uses his left hand to control the strap and his right hand to help her left forearm at the ledge. Check actual reach, slack and contact in the blocked scene, then revise the design if needed. These dimensions are scene-planning assumptions, not a real-world rigging specification.

Establish Miko's receiver during the early radio exchange. The parcel routes its future message privately to that receiver, audible to the audience but not Ren. He sees it activate and sees her expression change; this motivates his later question.

The six-second central action cut includes a brief crossing followed by a slip and struggle at the destination ledge; it does not mean a six-second ordinary jump across a small gap. Block the actual motion in the layout. Revise the action if the hand positions, tether or contacts cannot be made readable.

### English scene text

**REN:** Tell me that's our platform.

**MIKO:** It's a platform.

**REN:** That's not what I asked.

**OMI, RADIO:** Storm shutters closing. Find cover.

**MIKO:** That's the plan.

Miko makes the strap into a tether. Ren tests his end. The launch, slip and rescue play through action, breath and physical effort.

**REN, PULLING:** Got you.

**REN, RECOVERING:** You said that strap was useless.

**MIKO:** Only for carrying things.

The parcel activates Miko's private receiver. The audience hears Ren's voice through her listening perspective; present Ren cannot hear it and his living mouth is still.

**REN, FUTURE RECORDING:** Miko... don't let me go tomorrow.

Silence. Miko looks at the hand that just held her.

**REN:** What did it say?

**MIKO:** Wrong address.

**REN:** You're a terrible courier.

They head toward shelter. Her worry survives the joke.

**OMI, RADIO:** You two still there?

**MIKO:** Yeah. Both of us.

Title. The scene should make viewers curious about the relationship and the message, not require an explanation of the entire world.

## 3. What this pilot is testing

The script is intentionally compact enough to direct and revise. Its action has a prepared cause and consequence; the prop matters to the rescue and to the story. The same voice must read as ordinary Ren and a distressed recording of Ren. Miko's withheld information creates a character problem that can continue beyond the scene.

The target cue windows in pilot.json are planning data. Actual duration, selected take and generated media fields are null or empty. They are not subtitle timings or proof of a finished dub. The CSV mirrors those planned cues for a director/editor; it should be replaced or revised after recording.

Review the first animatic in three ways: normal picture and sound; picture without dialogue to assess geography and acting; and the English exchange without picture to assess intention and timing. These are proposed diagnostic passes, not separate release standards. A weakness in one pass should lead to a specific correction.

For C06-C08, prepare key poses and critical contact frames before a model is allowed to create motion. For C10-C11, prioritize the thought change and listening response. The quiet scene should not be built from an idle animation with generic mouth movement. Use generative assistance only where it survives the same review as the conventional shot.

## 4. A staged comparison that answers the speed question

Begin with three short tests totaling 28 seconds: a 12-second two-person exchange and prop handoff, an 8-second restrained reaction, and an 8-second action beat with contact and recovery. This is the minimum screen before attempting the 90-second pilot. It can reuse the pilot's original cast and set without claiming it is the same edit.

Compare an editable conventional baseline and at most two relevant AI routes per test. Give every route the same approved designs, audio, intent and required contacts. The inputs can be represented differently when a tool requires sketches, a pose video or a playblast; count the preparation labor rather than pretending those controls are free. Use model-compatible segment lengths and record unused handles.

A route that generates its own dialogue instead of consuming the approved track is not an equivalent fixed-audio test. Evaluate it separately or include replacement audio and synchronization repair in its measured work.

Cap the first comparison at three initial attempts per route and retain every result, including failures. After selecting a candidate, record the cleanup and revision time needed to meet the agreed standard. Distinguish accepted unchanged, accepted after repair, and rejected. Do not silently omit the cost of failed requests or unsuccessful cleanup.

Expand the test set to a difficult head turn, hand/prop transfer, identity through occlusion, changing lighting and an exact dialogue pickup only after the first screen produces a plausible candidate. Keep some scenes held out from tuning. A tool can win a narrow category and still be unsuitable for the rest of the show.

### The required ledger

| Record | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Provider/model/version, adapters, license snapshot | Makes the tested configuration identifiable |
| Exact source asset and audio hashes | Keeps comparisons tied to the same approved materials |
| GPU model/count, VRAM, precision, steps, resolution, fps | Makes a speed figure interpretable |
| Cold start, queue, inference, transfer, mux and review-ready times | Separates model speed from a usable delivery |
| All generated/processed duration and actual billable units | Captures retries, minimum clip lengths and audio-based charges |
| Selected timeline duration and selected take IDs | Prevents counting alternative or overlapping material twice |
| Artist, direction and review minutes | Captures the work needed to make a result acceptable |
| Fault list, repairs and reviewer decision for exact output | Explains why the apparent fast result did or did not work |

The blank benchmark template contains no observations. A result should only be added from actual job logs and a recorded review. Keep raw timings and invoices/usage records with the run, then derive summaries. Never fill a missing metric with an invented zero or estimate that looks measured.

### Quality before a speed winner

Require character identity, story-critical audio correctness, readable contacts, coherent camera geography and acceptable key-moment lip sync. Then compare overall acting, drawing, rhythm, sound and desire to continue watching. Automatic metrics can assist diagnosis; VBench-2.0 itself evaluates multiple distinct dimensions rather than reducing video quality to attractive individual frames. Stylized animation still needs its own human judgments. [R40]

Have an animator and sound/performance reviewer inspect their respective faults. Use a small randomized audience comparison for emotional clarity and interest, reporting the participant count and the exact prompt used. Do not claim a small panel proves popularity or that an LLM quality score proves a funny scene.

## 5. The revision experiment

Once one shot is accepted, request a single changed line and then a single changed gesture. Record which layers and frames must be rebuilt. Compare the output outside the intended change with the accepted version; inspect contact and sound boundaries. The winner must demonstrate useful correction, not only a good first sample.

Treat the dependency graph as an engineering design to verify. A new voice take may affect mouth timing, breath and acting. A longer take may shift editorial boundaries. A changed gesture may affect occlusion, shadows and a prop. A changed camera can affect nearly everything in that shot. Reusing a stale layer is not a speed improvement if it makes the scene wrong.

Store immutable selected source files, rendered layers and final media. Cache decisions should use content hashes and exact configuration. Keep approvals attached to the artifact version actually watched. A planned approval field in a JSON file is not a trusted authorization or an automatic creative evaluation.

## 6. Proposed engineering milestones

| Milestone | Concrete exit evidence |
|---|---|
| A. Directed voice test | Same original cast handles comedy, exertion and a quiet reversal; all selected audio retained |
| B. Editable ten-frame interchange | Color, alpha, exposure and audio timebase survive the chosen apps and export |
| C. 28-second comparison | Complete take ledger; quality review; measured correction and production time |
| D. Finished 90-second pilot | Full scene, final audio/mouth pass, all critical faults resolved, intended-viewer feedback |
| E. Three connected shorts | Character/voice continuity, consequences between episodes, measured reuse savings |
| F. Recurring production and live aftershow | Sustained accepted supply, buffer behavior, recovery test and an appropriately constrained live cast stage |

These milestones do not have promised calendar dates. Establish scheduling from the people, budget, GPU access and first measured production cycle. The highest-priority uncertainty is whether a complete scene works emotionally and can be corrected efficiently. Resolve that before expanding the cast, duration or live-generation demand.
