# Star Live: Creative Bible

**Development proposal · 19 October 2026**

*Night Window* is a working title and original concept for a possible Star AI animated series. The cast, visual direction, episode ideas and sample below are development material. They do not establish an approved show, finished assets, recorded voices, generated episodes, an operating channel or demonstrated television-level quality. This bible defines creative intent and review criteria; architecture, provider selection and deployment belong in the separate technical plan.

## 1. The show people would return for

An overnight lost-and-found receives everything a city has misplaced, including impossible things. Four coworkers must return each item before the morning trains while navigating their own unfinished relationships.

An umbrella containing its own weather can flood the station. The episode underneath that spectacle concerns someone taking a shortcut to seem useful. Everyday feelings give the impossible events consequences: embarrassment, jealousy, affection, disappointment and the pleasure of being understood.

The recurring story engine is a human want, an object with one understandable impossible property, a bad choice, escalating consequences and a costly better choice. The characters remember what happened. An apology can repair trust without erasing the reason it broke.

The proposed audience is older teens and adults who enjoy warm absurd comedy. Fix the content profile before production. A younger-audience series would need its own editorial decisions. References to admired cartoons express ambition; the production develops its own characters, settings, dialogue, music and performances.

The creative emphasis follows useful testimony from *Gumball* writers Jon Foster and James Lamont: emotional heart, writing for the characters and learning from audience reactions matter to their process. Their experience informs this proposal, rather than proving it will succeed. [BAFTA interview](https://www.bafta.org/stories/jon-foster-james-lamont-interview/)

## 2. Meet the cast

| Character | Desire and contradiction | Comedy and performance | Visual direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mara** | A gifted repairer wants to be indispensable but struggles to ask for help. | Economical speech; tries to make improvised rescues look planned. Small physical tells expose uncertainty. | Compact triangular silhouette, rolled orange work jacket, tool satchel, purposeful forward lean. |
| **Kit** | A returning performer wants approval and must learn to be dependable without applause. | Reads strangers better than friends. Commits to a terrible sales pitch until reality interrupts. | Rounded silhouette, pale green overshirt, expressive hands, loose movement that changes when embarrassed. |
| **Rook** | A veteran cleaner and staff representative protects the station but overcontrols it. | Unhurried and practical. Physically solves problems others keep discussing. Can be wrong without becoming foolish. | Broad grounded silhouette, indigo utility apron, deliberate stillness before decisive action. |
| **Bram** | An obsolete departure board rebuilt as a coworker fears becoming unnecessary. | Warm precision; rehearses spontaneous remarks. His split-flap signs betray reactions he tries to conceal. | Tall narrow sign body, hinged feet, folding arms, consistent lettering and readable poses. |

Mara and Kit are childhood friends whose broken promise predates the pilot. Rook challenges Mara's refusal to delegate and Kit's unreliability. Kit encourages Bram to improvise; Bram notices when Kit avoids responsibility. Give each pairing a different rhythm and each character interests beyond the station. Nobody exists solely to deliver sarcasm.

Voice briefs describe intention, rhythm, range and pronunciation. Mara lands conclusions quickly; Kit keeps reshaping a sentence to win someone over; Rook allows silence; Bram sounds warmly earnest. Develop original performances using appropriately licensed voices. Real guests participate through explicit opt-in arrangements covering their likeness, voice and intended use.

## 3. Visual and performance language

Use hand-inked 2D characters against painted station backgrounds: warm work lights, deep night blues, worn enamel, glass and practical signage. The public counter and storage maze are the primary sets; the platform is an occasional third location. Their geography should remain understandable through entrances, eye-lines and prop placement.

Approve model sheets, expression ranges, costume details, scale comparisons and character-specific movement before episode production. Rig reuse supports consistency; memorable poses, anticipation, held reactions and motivated camera choices provide variety. Bram's typography must remain legible and consistent.

Stage comedy visually. A listener noticing something can matter more than the speaker's next line. Keep music away from a reveal that needs silence. Spend detailed animation on meaningful acting and action beats.

Storyboard artist Tim Hodge describes rough thumbnails, camera placement, reaction shots and revising an opening after seeing the ending. His process supports an early, inexpensive stage for discovering problems. [Toon Boom interview](https://www.toonboom.com/tim-hodge-on-storyboarding-for-tv-animation)

## 4. Story and comedy house rules

- Begin with what a character wants today and why another character makes it difficult.
- Establish an object's rule through action. Escalation must follow that rule; a new unexplained power cannot conveniently resolve the climax.
- Let choices cause consequences. Coincidence can introduce trouble, but the cast earns the resolution.
- Build setups, reversals, visual discoveries and callbacks. Remove explanations that repeat what viewers just understood.
- Vary comic rhythm. Quiet reactions and sincere moments need space; a fixed joke quota would distort them.
- Give every scene a purpose and a change. Enter late, leave when the beat lands, and preserve needed context.
- Write a satisfying season ending before planting its central mystery. Track each clue, reveal dependency and character's knowledge.
- Evaluate originality across premises, phrasing, staging and designs. A renamed familiar cast is insufficient development.

The proposed seasonal question is why unclaimed objects arrive bearing tomorrow's date and the crew's names. Settle its explanation before production. Each episode must remain enjoyable for viewers who never investigate that mystery.

## 5. Three episode premises

**A Minute to Spare.** Mara borrows lost minutes to complete every repair without help. She must repay them precisely when her coworkers need her. Her competence remains valuable, but the resolution requires an honest handoff. A recurring visual beat jumps from her heroic preparation to the unfinished aftermath everyone else encounters.

**Standing Ovation.** Kit uses unclaimed applause to impress a former friend. It begins rewarding the wrong things, while Bram struggles to distinguish sincere approval from noise. Kit finally does a needed favor in silence. The applause belongs to the fictional plot and must never be represented as actual viewer response.

**The Last Departure.** Bram discovers his route has disappeared from the new timetable and makes the station increasingly rigid to prove his usefulness. His improvements strand someone whose trip cannot fit a schedule. Rook recognizes a similar fear behind her own routines. A recovered ticket references a platform that should not exist.

## 6. Cold open: “Weather Report”

**Target length: 60–90 seconds, subject to the timed animatic.**

INT. NIGHT WINDOW — NIGHT.

A ceiling drips into a bucket. Mara balances on a stool beneath it. Rook quietly moves her sandwich away from the splash.

Kit enters carrying a beautiful, battered umbrella.

**KIT:** Good news. Found something useful.

**MARA:** A roofer?

Kit opens the umbrella indoors. A small rain cloud opens beneath it. Rain pours directly onto Kit.

Everyone watches long enough for him to decide whether to pretend this was intentional.

**KIT:** Portable weather.

**ROOK:** Take it outside.

Kit goes to the door. The umbrella is wider than the doorway. He turns sideways. It isn't.

Bram's flaps quietly change from DEPARTURES to DELAYS. He looks embarrassed and changes them back.

Mara steps off the stool, takes the umbrella, and folds it. The rain stops. Kit brightens.

Mara turns the umbrella point-down over the bucket and begins fastening it shut with a cable tie.

One small fish drops into the bucket.

They all look down.

**MARA:** Where did you find this?

Kit slowly looks toward the storage room. Behind its frosted window, a second umbrella opens. A flash of sunlight. Then a very large shadow passes through it.

Rook picks up her sandwich.

**ROOK:** I'm taking my break now.

TITLE.

Review whether the holds, doorway business and involuntary sign change read without explanation. The shadow provides a final escalation without requiring a detailed creature reveal. This sample needs performance and audience testing.

## 7. “Window Still Open”: the live aftershow

A proposed two-to-four-minute segment after each episode lets the cast address viewers directly. Label it as an aftershow and keep its memory separate from story canon. D23's in-character Grunkle Stan fan interview offers an example of maintaining a comic personality while handling questions and protecting story surprises. [D23 interview](https://d23.com/gravity-falls-creatorexecutive-producer-alex-hirsch-grunkle-stan-answer-d23s-questions/)

The host selects and deduplicates suitable questions. Audience submissions are untrusted input, with no authority to change tools, permissions, publication settings or the show bible. Replies use aired episodes and approved public character briefs. Future scripts and hidden answers stay outside that retrieval scope.

Characters can discuss preferences, relationships already shown and events viewers just watched. Spoiler requests receive brief in-character deflections. Newly improvised facts become candidates for editorial review, not immediate canon. An approved reveal ledger controls subsequent scripts and cast biographies.

Audience choices may influence a later unlocked episode or a bounded alternative prepared for that interaction. Preserve already approved episodes. If nobody is chatting, use a short prepared cast bit and continue; never fabricate viewer questions. Distinguish genuine live responses, prepared segments and replays accurately.

## 8. Production and quality review

The showrunner selects an emotional question using the bible, recent episodes, available assets, runtime and production allowance. Develop several premises with different conflicts and visual opportunities. A story editor selects one; a continuity editor checks chronology, character knowledge and object rules before the outline is approved.

Next come dialogue, a scratch performance, rough boards and a timed animatic. Review those before final rendering. Fix a confusing beat at its source rather than polishing around it. Detailed outline control has improved human ratings of coherence in prose-story research; that is relevant evidence for planning, not a validation of animated comedy. [DOC research](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.10077)

Review character truth, narrative clarity, humor and timing, visual acting, performance and audio, continuity, and accessibility separately. Identify exact failed beats and repair proposals. Do not average away a serious defect or interpret a model score as proof that viewers laughed.

Calibrate with consenting target viewers using blinded, randomized rough-animatic comparisons. Ask what confused them, what they remember, which moment they would share and whether they want the next episode. Preserve disagreement. Research documents position bias in LLM judges, supporting caution with automated comparisons. [ACL research](https://aclanthology.org/2025.ijcnlp-long.18/)

Freeze approved media and referenced asset versions before broadcast. Afterwards, combine voluntary feedback, retention and moderated chat reactions. Account for stream delay and repeated messages. A chat spike can suggest a clip candidate; the editor still checks its setup, payoff and context. Proposed improvements remain versioned changes rather than automatic personality rewrites. Any automated scoring derived from YouTube API data follows the permissions and retention rules in the YouTube integration appendix.

## 9. Companion website specification

**Watch.** Lead with the current video, accurate live/replay/offline state, episode title and next aftershow time. Keep captions, volume and playback controls discoverable. Differentiate joining live from starting an available episode from its beginning. Show only measured audience figures.

**Meet the Cast.** Present a strong portrait, short personality-led biography, relationships, user-triggered voice sample and favorite-scene link for each character. Distinguish the fictional character from performer and production credits. An episode-based spoiler setting limits biographies to aired knowledge.

**Episode Archive.** Organize by season and episode, with readable summaries, runtime, content notes, captions, descriptive transcripts and continue-watching state. Thumbnails and titles also obey spoiler preferences. Aftershow recordings remain clearly identified.

**Clips and World.** Highlights preserve their setup and link to their source episodes. A station map and recovered objects deepen the world with clues already aired. Provide a linear text alternative. Hidden future answers must not appear in downloaded page data.

**Mobile and chat.** Put the player first and chat in a separate collapsible region. Provide a clear question-submission state without covering captions. If embedded chat is unavailable, offer a direct “Open chat on YouTube” link while preserving the viewing page. Do not require a second account simply to duplicate platform chat.

**About and accessibility.** Explain AI involvement, creative intent, credits and guest participation. Include keyboard access, visible focus, readable contrast, touch-friendly controls, reduced motion and ways to pause or hide updating chat. Avoid autoplay audio and hover-only actions. Plan captions, transcripts and visual descriptions during production, following [W3C media guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/); provide controls for [parallel updating content](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/pause-stop-hide.html). Test the finished experience before claiming accessibility conformance.

## 10. Expansion to anime

Develop anime as a separate editorial and production profile with its own cast, expression sheets, key poses, serialized causality, action geography and compositing rules. First establish whether a short *Night Window* pilot supports memorable performances and repeat viewing. Expansion should follow demonstrated creative appeal and measured production capacity.
